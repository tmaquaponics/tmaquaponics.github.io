### Introduction

Neocaridina shrimp is one of the most popular pet to keep in the hobby. In this article, we will explore its diet, breeding and general care.

### Overview

![Burried blue dream shrimp](/assets/img/blue-dream-velvet-neocaridina-davidi.jpg)
*Burried blue dream shrimp*

Neocardinia is a freshwater shrimp measuring up to 3 cm. It is a detritivore, meaning, its diet is composed of detritus and decaying matter. It can live up to 2 years in optimal conditions, and comes in many colors and variations. Among the most popular varieties, we can cite red cherry shrimp, blue dream shrimp and golden back yellow shrimp.
<!--more-->
### Diet

Neocaridina will eat everything that is decaying and can grow biofilm on. 

### Breeding

Neocaridina shrimp breed every other month, with females carrying the eggs under their belly for about a month. 

A Female signals that it is ready to breed by releasing pheromones that attract males. This usually occurs after the female molts, which is the time its shell is still soft. A male attracted by these pheromones will chase the female, mount on top of a her and then proceeds to fertilize her.

At first, the female carries its eggs first behind its head. This clutch of eggs is called a saddle (like a horse saddle). Once fertilized, the eggs then migrate from behind the head to the under-flaps, where they will grow until they hatch. The female keeps the eggs oxygenated by constantly moving its under-flaps.

When the time has come to release the eggs, the female looks for a safe place like a clump of java moss and releases its eggs. Baby shrimps, a miniature version of their parents, will remain in the same place the first few days, which is why they should have some biofilm to munch on. 

**Selective breeding** [1]

Selective breeding is the process of breeding neocaridina shrimp to produce specific traits. 
To get the pattern and color you want, you can breed the children with their parents to fortify a dominant gene or get rid of a recessive gene. However, too much inbreeding can slow the breeding rate and cause problems in the line to show up.  Problems like a short nose, a small body, or too weak shrimps. This usually happens around the sixth generation.

To counteract inbreeding problems, master breeders do the breeding in a controlled manner. They start from a single line 'group A' that is divided into three groups, A1, A2, and A3, all children of group A. Each group is placed in its own tank, T1, T2 and T3 respectively.

The children of A1 are in turn placed in the tank T4, the children of A2 in tank T5, and the children of A3 in the tank T6. Once matured, all the males from T4 are then placed in tank T2, and all the female in tank T3. From Tank T5, all the females go to tank T1, and all the males go to tank T3. From tank T6, all the males go to tank T1 and all the females go to tank T2.

The objective of this crossing is to cut the inbreeding between brother and sister shrimps.

After a certain time, one should also consider breeding with a line from a supplier to mix up genetics and reduce the inbreeding problems.

**Culling**

Culling refers to the process of taking out from the tank shrimps that do not posses the traits we are looking for, and therefore should not be allowed to breed with our line. Culls usually have dull colors, malformations, or are simply week.

### General care

**Feeding**

To feed your shrimp, place some dry leaves in the tank as a constant source of food. Examples include loquat laves, which decay really slowly, almond leaves and various aquarium safe cones.

Feed them fish food once a day. Blanched vegetables like cucumber and spinach every other day. Nettle and mulberry leaves (they absolutely love them) as much as you can.

**Tank mates**

The best tank mates for neocaridnia shrimp are Asian trumpet snails. Because they will eat excess food and bury themselves in the substrate. This burring prevents the formation of pockets of gas. I don't personally recommend ramshorn snails but they are also a popular choice to keep with neocaridina. I do however recommend bladder snails as I see them as less invasive than ramsohorn snails.

**Tank setup**

The best setup to start a thriving neocaridina colony is about 15 shrimp in a 20 gallons tank, equipped with a sponge and an undergravel filter, with a temperature of 22 to 24 Celsius. 

The most important aspect of the setup is algae and biofilm, so make sure that green walls are well established in the tank. The best plant to keep with neocaridina shrimp is java moss. They absolutely love it as it provides a source of food and cover. They love to graze on it and it is perfect for adult and baby shrimps to hide in.

### Conclusion

We have briefly explored the Neocaridina shrimp profile and how you can keep this type of shrimp. Many varieties exist in the wild and in the hobby. With the information provided here, you can be confident in keeping them. 

Happy shrimpkeeping !!
